Research

Generative AI has entered classrooms and workplaces faster than the preparation of the people who teach and train in them. My research asks how educators, trainers, and learners can keep human judgment in the loop, and I pursue that question along three connected lines.

Generative AI and critical thinking

With colleagues, I conducted a bibliometric analysis and systematic review of research on GenAI and critical thinking in higher education, published in the Journal of Computers in Education (2026). In a companion study in Education Sciences (2025), I used the Practical Inquiry model of the Community of Inquiry framework to contrast passive, AI-directed use with human–AI supported collaboration. That article has been cited 171 times on Google Scholar, and its findings are available as an interactive research visualization.

Triggering Event (Ask)

From action (practice) toward perception (awareness)

  • Identify a problem or gap in understanding.
  • Write a focused question.
  • Enter the question as a prompt to GenAI.

Exploration (Engage)

From perception (awareness) toward deliberation (applicability)

  • Review the AI response.
  • Ask follow-up questions or request clarifications and examples.

Integration (Evaluate)

From deliberation (applicability) toward conception (ideas)

  • Compare GenAI responses with prior knowledge and other credible sources.
  • Check for accuracy, logic, and ethical alignment.

Resolution (Reflect)

From conception (ideas) back to action (practice)

  • Contextualize the GenAI-generated content.
  • Decide how to use the generated content.
  • Apply it to real life.

These studies led me to design a structured critical AI inquiry model for college students. When I implemented it in online courses, academic integrity concerns in student writing fell from high to low levels, and in bilingual and multicultural education it led to more responsible use and greater ethical awareness.

The systematic review in the Journal of Computers in Education proposes PAEERA, a six-step instructional model that carries the same human-in-the-loop logic into everyday academic work.

Plan

Set goals and build AI literacy

Identify what you need, set clear goals, and build AI literacy: how AI works, its opportunities, limitations, and responsible use.

Ask

Use the rhetorical situation

Write purposeful, context-specific prompts using elements of the rhetorical situation: audience, genre, purpose, subject, context, exigence, and writer.

Explore

Iterate and refine

Engage in back-and-forth with AI by asking follow-ups, requesting examples, and refining ideas.

Evaluate

Compare and contrast

Examine AI output against what you know, course materials, scholarly sources, and alternative viewpoints.

Reflect

Use the rhetorical triangle for ethical use

Apply the rhetorical triangle (ethos, logos, pathos) to judge credibility, logic, bias, and needed adjustments.

Apply

Use insights authentically

Adapt, integrate, or reject AI-generated content using your own judgment, voice, and disciplinary expectations.

PAEERA: Plan, Ask, Explore, Evaluate, Reflect, and Apply, a six-step instructional model for critically applying generative AI in academic work, from planning and prompt writing to authentic application. From Nasr et al. (2026), Journal of Computers in Education. Read the articleView the original figure

Through IGNITE-STEM, an NSF FINDERS Foundry project with Tuba City Unified School District on the Navajo Nation, our team co-designed an Ask, Explore, Check, Decide model for AI-supported STEM learning with teachers, families, and community members. We are now validating a critical thinking survey for K–12 STEM students who learn with AI.

Measuring multidimensional AI literacy

AI professional development is often designed without evidence of what educators already know. I developed the Functional, Ethical, Rhetorical, and Pedagogical AI Literacy Scale for Educators (FERPAI), grounded in digital multiliteracies theory and UNESCO’s AI Competency Framework for Teachers. In an initial validation with 239 preservice, K–12, and higher-education educators, the four subscales were reliable (α = .87–.93) and the refined model showed acceptable fit (CFI = .920, SRMR = .059).

Functional AI Literacy (Acquire)

Educator as effective operator

  • Training data and bias
  • Troubleshoot errors
  • Strengths and limitations
  • Customize tools
  • Stay updated

Technical understanding informs ethical judgment.

Ethical AI Literacy (Deepen)

Educator as informed questioner

  • Explore ethics research
  • Co-construct guidelines
  • Analyze ethical issues
  • Follow policies, credit, and disclosure

Ethical judgment guides responsible use.

Rhetorical AI Literacy (Deepen → Create)

Educator as reflective producer

  • Rhetorical situation
  • Prompt strategies
  • Rhetorical triangle and contextual fit
  • Calibrate reliance and authorship

Contextual evaluation informs instructional design.

Pedagogical AI Literacy (Create)

Educator as instructional designer and creator

  • Apply learning theories
  • Integrate for critical thinking, inclusion, and engagement
  • Build activities
  • Adjust methods and assessment
  • Evaluate learning effects
  • Reflect for professional growth

Reflection and professional growth renew the cycle.

Four interrelated literacies organized along UNESCO’s Acquire, Deepen, Create progression and integrated through human-in-the-loop oversight, with the educator as agent, evaluator, and decision-maker. Grounded in UNESCO’s AI Competency Framework for Teachers (Miao & Cukurova, 2024) and Selber’s digital multiliteracies (2004). Designing AI-enhanced professional learning

My dissertation, GenAI Literacy for Language Teachers for a More Responsible and Effective Use of the Technology: Testing, Experiences, and Reflections, is a sequential mixed-methods study of the Teachers in the Loop training. I designed it the way an instructional designer designs workplace training: a baseline needs assessment, then ten microlearning videos, narrated modules, and an avatar-based facilitator for asynchronous, low-bandwidth delivery. Teachers improved significantly on all four literacy dimensions (Journal of Applied Instructional Design, 2026). A companion study examines teachers’ engagement with synthetic presenters.

Methods I use and teach
  • Reflection on experience, following Dewey: process before product
  • Modeling and gradual release (I do, we do, you do) with quick, specific feedback
  • Community of Inquiry design for teaching, social, and cognitive presence online
  • Online literature circles, reflective matrices, and portfolios
  • Universal Design for Learning, with more than one way to engage and to show learning
  • Human-in-the-loop use of generative AI, guided by a critical thinking framework
  • Classroom observation and targeted coaching for preservice teachers
  • Personal, reflective video feedback
